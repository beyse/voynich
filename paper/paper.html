Constrained, not identifiable: statistical constraints on the production of the Voynich manuscript text

Sebastian Beyer

Independent researcher, Vienna, Austria. Correspondence: @BeyerSebastian on X.

Abstract. Which production mechanisms are compatible with the text of the Voynich manuscript, and can text statistics decide whether its free choices carry a message? An exploratory analysis shows that ordering information between tokens sits in the glyphs at the token boundary, that line breaks interrupt it, that real tokens are attested no more often than tokens sampled from a glyph model, and that the text carries no measurable information about the colours of its drawings. A low-order glyph process with drifting parameters reproduces much of this profile on held-out pages; published generators and the Naibbe cipher do not. We then applied a battery of calibrated tests, specified before the Voynich outcomes were computed. Its controls are handwritten human gibberish, natural languages, historical-style ciphers and generators with known mechanisms. A sequence of further pre-specified experiments followed. Nine measured properties constrain the mechanism, among them rigid slot order within words, margin-associated line-final forms, short-range recency across line and paragraph boundaries, and very low recurrence of locally deviant forms. They exclude naive improvisation, the tested stationary procedures without a recency state, settings that switch per line or paragraph, copy-and-modify with propagating innovations, the tested natural-language controls, the Naibbe cipher as published, and copying from a re-lined draft. The best explicit generator reproduces 33 of 40 statistics on held-out pages. Finally, arithmetic decoding lets the free choices of a Voynich-like process carry about 44 kB of compressed plaintext per manuscript, recovered without error. In 20 runs per condition, also when the message drives the best explicit generator, no statistic rejected equality with randomly driven output after Holm correction, and all satisfied a pre-specified tolerance criterion. The production mechanism is constrained, but whether its free choices carry a message is not identifiable from the text alone.

Keywords: Voynich manuscript; mutual information; mechanism identification; human gibberish; Naibbe cipher; mimic functions; non-identifiability

1. Introduction

The Voynich manuscript is a codex on vellum radiocarbon-dated to the early fifteenth century (Zandbergen n.d.). It contains about 38,000 word tokens in an unidentified script, written by several scribes (Currier 1976; Davis 2020). A century of decipherment attempts has not produced an accepted reading (D'Imperio 1978; Bowern and Lindemann 2021). Quantitative work has described its unusual properties: two statistical "languages" (Currier 1976), low conditional glyph entropy and a rigid word structure (Stolfi 2000; Lindemann and Bowern 2020; Zattera 2022), strong coupling between the last glyph of one word and the first of the next (Rozanova and Temerev 2026), and a positional entropy profile shared with some verbose ciphers (Kinnison 2026). Constructive proposals have shown that such properties can arise from a table and grille (Rugg 2004), from copying and modifying earlier words (Timm and Schinner 2020), from a historically executable cipher (Greshko 2025), or from human gibberish (Gaskell and Bowern 2022). Bowern and Gaskell (2022) showed that several encipherment methods, among them encoding letters as bigraphs, merging phonemic contrasts and adding nulls, bring character-level statistics of Latin close to Voynich values, although no single method matched on all of their metrics. Hermes (2022) showed that the Polygraphia III of Johannes Trithemius (1506), which replaces each plaintext letter with an invented word, approaches the Voynich difference between unigram and conditional glyph entropy, its word-length distributions and its many near-identical neighbouring words when the substitution columns are chosen at random.

Results of this kind establish sufficiency: a mechanism can produce the text. They do not establish identity, because the same statistics are compatible with a procedure followed by rule, with a practised writer's habits, and with a cipher whose free choices are driven by a message. This paper replaces the question "which mechanism fits?" with two others. First, which mechanisms does the text exclude, under tests whose ability to discriminate has been shown on controls with known mechanisms? Second, is there any statistical route from the text alone to the question of whether a message is present?

We answer both in two parts. The first part (Section 3) is exploratory. It applies one battery of information-theoretic statistics, with marginal-preserving null models, to the manuscript, to language controls and to candidate generators, and shows that a local glyph process reproduces most of that profile. It was not specified in advance, and it shaped the hypotheses of the second part. In the second part every analysis was specified in a written plan, committed to the project repository before its Voynich outcome was computed (Section 4.3). A battery of seven tests (T1–T7) was calibrated on controls first and then applied to the manuscript. Further experiments (E1–E8) were designed to separate the mechanisms the battery left standing, to build explicit generators of the surviving class and test them on held-out pages, and to test constructively whether a message can hide in the free choices.

The contributions are:

  1. An exploratory profile of the text: ordering information carried by the glyphs at token boundaries rather than by whole tokens, a lexicon test in which real tokens are attested no more often than tokens sampled from a glyph model, a sufficient local glyph process tested on held-out pages, the characteristic failures of published generators and of the Naibbe cipher, and a text–illustration test with calibrated sensitivity.
  2. A two-axis hypothesis-separation framework: generation by fixed procedure, skilled improvisation or plaintext-driven procedure, crossed with direct composition or copying. Every discriminator has predictions and falsifiers stated in advance and a calibration requirement on controls.
  3. A mechanism class defined by nine measured properties, each with its evidence and an explicit strength label (Table 8). Several are new empirical findings: line-final forms associated with the margin and much weaker at paragraph ends, recency that runs continuously across line and paragraph boundaries, very low recurrence of locally deviant forms, page clustering even of the most frequent forms, and within-page drift that is lexical in type.
  4. Exclusions of named mechanism variants, kept separate from the alternatives that remain open (Table 9).
  5. Held-out tests of explicit generators with ablations, which identify necessary components and the properties no tested generator reproduces.
  6. A constructive non-identifiability result for this text. The general principle, that a message can drive the random choices of a known process without changing its output distribution, is established (Wayner 1992; Cachin 2004; Hopper, Langford and von Ahn 2002). What is new is its instantiation for a Voynich-like process on the manuscript's layout, the measured capacity of that process (1.90 bits per glyph), and its evaluation against the full 40-statistic phenotype, with 20 runs per condition and for two processes. Text statistics therefore cannot settle the plaintext question without further assumptions about the encoder.

The strongest conclusion the evidence supports is this: a set of production constraints can be recovered from the text, but message-carrying and message-free processes of the constrained class are not identifiable from the text alone. Nothing in this paper shows that the Voynich text is meaningless, and nothing decodes it.

2. Materials

2.1 Transliterations and units

The primary transliteration is ZL3b (Zandbergen and Landini 2025) in the IVTFF format. We use paragraph text (loci of type P), join uncertain spaces (commas; the alternative is a robustness check), and drop tokens containing unreadable or rare-glyph codes. This leaves 32,428 tokens on 207 pages. In the exploratory profile (Section 3) a glyph is a single EVA character. The pre-specified tests segment glyphs into units, with the multigraphs cth, ckh, cph, cfh, ch, sh as single units. The Takahashi transliteration IT2a (34,411 tokens) is aligned by page and line. A token is double-coded when both transliterations read it identically in lines with equal token counts. Page metadata give the quire, the illustration section, the Currier language and the scribal hand, following Davis (2020). A line is paragraph-final if it carries the paragraph-end marker, otherwise margin-bound. Page order is the current binding order.

2.2 Controls

Every test is calibrated on corpora whose mechanism is known.

  • Human gibberish: the 38 handwritten documents collected by Gaskell and Bowern (2022), 10,075 tokens in lowercase Latin letters with the writers' line breaks preserved. Each writer invented a different "language".
  • Natural language: Caesar's De bello Gallico (Latin), Dante's Commedia (Italian), Goethe's Faust (German) and Tolstoy's War and Peace in English, cut into the Voynich page and line shape. The profile adds consonantal Hebrew (Genesis) as an abjad with short words. Latin is also wrapped greedily at a fixed width, as a re-lined text for the line tests.
  • Ciphers: the Naibbe cipher (Greshko 2025), reimplemented from the published tables, checked against the author's own published ciphertext (Section 3.3), and enciphering Latin as running text and as independent lines, with state-dependent variants; a verbose substitution with deterministic homophones; and ciphertext-feedback substitution.
  • Synthetic generators:
    • an order-3 glyph automaton per language and section, with and without page drift;
    • a table and grille after Rugg (2004);
    • copy-and-modify after Timm and Schinner (2020);
    • a periodic device with column and row effects of adjustable strength;
    • generators with smooth or stepwise parameter drift, settings per line or paragraph, explicit recency, and vocabulary or glyph-habit drift.
    Generators fitted to or built from Voynich statistics are used only to show how a test responds to a known mechanism, never as evidence of fit.

2.3 Illustration features

For the text–illustration test we computed ten colour features of each page from the Beinecke digital facsimile of folios 1r–58v: the area shares, centroids and hue of green, blue and red–brown pixels, classified relative to the page's parchment colour (Supplement S1). They are crude proxies for leaf mass, flowers and roots, chosen to test one hypothesis: that the text of a herbal page describes visible properties of its drawing.

2.4 Held-out split

For generator tests the manuscript is split by the parity of the bifolio index within each quire. Conjugate leaves stay together, and every quire and the three main hands occur in both halves. Generators see only the training half's text, plus the layout and metadata of test pages (line lengths, paragraph ends, quire, section, language).

3. An exploratory profile of the text

Before the pre-specified program we applied one battery of statistics to the manuscript, to the language controls and to candidate generators. This analysis was exploratory: it was not specified in advance, and its results shaped the hypotheses of Section 4. It establishes the properties the later tests build on. Its main result, that a local glyph process reproduces most of this profile, is the starting point for asking what the text excludes. Methods are summarised here and given in full in Supplement S1. All tables of this section are in Supplement S2.

3.1 Measurements

A token is a string between spaces; inner tokens are neither first nor last in their line. Lines with fewer than two tokens are dropped, which leaves 32,399 tokens. Mutual information (MI) is estimated by plug-in and is interpreted only as the excess over a null model that preserves the marginals exactly, because the plug-in bias depends on sample size and marginals (Paninski 2003). Order within lines is tested against a permutation of the inner tokens of each line. Pairs that span lines, and pairs of token and page, are tested against a re-pairing null. Corpora are compared at equal numbers of pairs, and the controls are cut into the line, paragraph and page lengths of the Voynich text. Uncertainty is a leave-one-quire-out jackknife over 16 quires.

3.2 Five properties

Ordering information sits at the token boundary (Figure 1a; Table S2). Adjacent inner tokens share 0.076 bit of excess MI as whole tokens, but the last glyph of one and the first glyph of the next share 0.146 bit. In the languages the order is reversed. Whole tokens share 0.178 (Hebrew) to 0.409 bit (Italian), and the boundary glyphs 0.037 (Latin) to 0.152 bit (Italian, whose elisions couple word edges). A projection of a pair cannot carry more information than the pair, so the token-level estimate is attenuated by sparsity. The finding concerns where the information is carried, not how much there is. The coupling is stronger in Currier B (0.187) than in Currier A (0.082) and agrees with Rozanova and Temerev (2026).

The excess lies in pairs of similar tokens, not in collocations of dissimilar words. Pairs at edit distance 3 or more carry 0.243 of Latin's 0.262 bit but −0.075 of the Voynich text's 0.203 bit (Table S23). This similarity of neighbours reflects the page, not copying from the line above. The closest of the three tokens above a token lies within edit distance 1 as often as the closest of three random tokens of the page (0.108 against 0.109; Table S22).

Line breaks interrupt the coupling (Table S3). The last token of a line and the first token of the next share 0.005 bit, against 0.062 ± 0.007 for adjacent pairs at the same n. At the glyph level the values are 0.012 and 0.144. In the language controls the two are equal (Latin 0.077 and 0.070), and even Latin written as one independent sentence per line keeps 0.159 bit across the break. The page-conditional estimator of Section 5 (T1), the only one we found valid under writer heterogeneity, puts the ratio at 0.22 with a standard error of 0.25. The coupling is therefore strongly reduced at line breaks. Whether it is absent is not decided.

Token boundaries are predictable from the flanking glyphs (Table S4). One glyph on each side gives an information gain of 73.7% about whether a boundary is present (F1 = 0.85), against 38.7% to 44.9% in the languages. Latin stripped of its spaces and re-spaced by a fixed glyph rule gives 73.2%.

There is no measurable lexicon (Figure 1b; Table S5). A within-token glyph trigram model is fitted on a random half of the tokens. Tokens sampled from it are attested in that half as often as held-out real tokens (0.76 against 0.79), and the jackknife interval of the gap includes zero. In every language, and in Latin cut into rule-spaced pieces or syllable pairs, sampled tokens are attested far less often (Latin 0.20 against 0.81; rule-spaced Latin 0.28 against 0.62). As far as this test can tell, a Voynich token is whatever the local glyph statistics produce.

The entropy profile is that of a low-order automaton (Table S6). With four symbols of context, the conditional entropy of the glyph stream is only −0.06 bit below that of an order-3 twin of the same size. The languages lie −0.30 (Latin) to −0.13 bit (Hebrew) below theirs. Under lzma compression, which sees all ranges, the residual of the text against a twin whose order-3 parameters drift by section is −0.039 bit per glyph. Samples of the automaton itself give +0.033 and −0.030; Latin gives −0.397.

Table 1 applies the battery to candidate generators. All of them produce text in the exact line and token shape of the Voynich paragraph text. A glyph automaton of order 3 is fitted per Currier language and section, treats the space as a symbol, and is sampled line by line. It reproduces the boundary coupling (0.167 against 0.146), the line-break reset, the boundary predictability, the hapax share (0.71 against 0.71), the lexicon test and the residual. Its token MI (0.050 against 0.078) and its page locality (0.134 against 0.232) fall short. Letting its parameters drift by page closes both gaps (0.076, 0.215), but that variant is fitted in-sample.

Out of sample, an automaton fitted per section on the odd-numbered pages matches the held-out even pages in boundary coupling (0.150 against 0.150), hapax share and the lexicon test, with residuals of −0.030 (order 3), +0.020 (order 4) and +0.010 bit per glyph (halves swapped). It misses only the token MI (0.007 against 0.034) and the page locality (0.168 against 0.289), which is the page-level variation a section-level model cannot contain (Table S11). The profile holds on the Takahashi transliteration (boundary MI 0.165), with uncertain spaces treated as spaces (0.182), and in each of the three main hands (0.079, 0.195, 0.185; Tables S10–S12).

The other mechanisms fail in characteristic ways.

  • Table and grille; copy-and-modify. Both choose the parts of neighbouring words independently, so they have almost no boundary coupling (0.006 and 0.010 bit). Table reuse and copying create long-range repetition that the compressor finds (residuals −0.766 and −1.030).
  • Verbose cipher with fixed homophones. It leaks the collocations of its plaintext (token MI 1.237) and has few hapaxes (0.30).
  • Ciphertext-feedback substitution, the class that can in principle imitate a Markov chain, overshoots the boundary coupling (0.649), makes spaces unpredictable (36.5%) and keeps the redundancy of its plaintext (residual −0.310).
  • The Naibbe cipher is the strongest competitor. It matches the entropy profile, the space predictability (77.4%) and, unlike every other plaintext mechanism, the residual (−0.065): the entropy of its card draws hides the plaintext's redundancy from the compressor. It fails the boundary coupling by a factor of about fifty (0.003), the hapax share (0.49, from an inventory closed at 5,978 types) and the page locality (0.007). Our reimplementation reproduces the author's own implementation. Enciphering the author's tokenised plaintext, 100.0% of his 34,764 published ciphertext tokens are valid outputs of the tables, the table shares lie within 0.014 of the deck proportions, and 7 of 8 statistics agree with his output; only the hapax share differs slightly (0.505 against 0.518). On the author's own ciphertext, the boundary MI is 0.0010 and the page MI 0.000 (Tables S36 and S37).
  • State-dependent Naibbe. With probability w the next table is chosen from the last glyph of the previous token. This creates boundary coupling but costs key entropy. At w = 1.0 the coupling (0.255) exceeds the manuscript's, while the token MI rises to 0.342 and the residual to −0.222. At w = 0.9 the coupling is still too small (0.085) and the token MI (0.181) already too large. For a cipher of this kind, boundary coupling and hidden plaintext redundancy compete. For a glyph automaton without plaintext they do not.

The table-and-grille and copy-and-modify generators are our reconstructions of the published descriptions; unlike the Naibbe cipher, they could not be checked against their authors' outputs. Their failures follow from the constructions (independent choice of neighbouring boundary glyphs; long-range reuse), not from parameter settings.

3.4 Drift, scribal hand and the illustrations

Drift and hand. The glyph-bigram distributions of two pages of the same section and Currier language diverge more the farther apart the pages are in the binding. In Currier A the Jensen–Shannon divergence rises from 0.124 at one or two pages to 0.161 beyond forty, while a twin with one model per section stays flat (0.093 to 0.094; Table S13). Sections occupy contiguous folio ranges, so section and drift are confounded. In the one section written by several hands (Herbal-B, 32 pages), pages at most six folios apart differ by 0.138 when written by the same hand and by 0.182 when written by different hands (Table S14). At fixed section, language and distance, the hand effect exceeds the section effect, which makes scribal practice a plausible source of the drift. That hands and Currier languages coincide is known (Currier 1976; Davis 2020); the controlled comparison is new.

Text and illustration. If the text of a herbal page described its drawing, pages with similar drawings should carry similar text. We compared text distance and colour-feature distance between pages with a Mantel test (Mantel 1967), excluding pairs closer than three folios. For the 85 Herbal-A pages of hand 1 the correlation is r = −0.055 (p = 0.79) for token frequencies and r = −0.070 (p = 0.87) for glyph bigrams. The same text distance does detect the folio drift (r = +0.115, p = 0.001; Table S15). A positive control adds to each page k pseudo-tokens that are deterministic functions of its features; the test detects them from k = 10 (r = +0.195, p = 0.001; Table S16), about 11% of a page's tokens. An unstratified correlation in Herbal-B (p = 0.046) is carried by the hand and disappears when the permutation is stratified within hand (p = 0.076). The test therefore excludes descriptions in which about a tenth or more of a page's tokens are determined by the measured colours. It says nothing about content that is not visible in the colours, such as names or uses.

Labels. Label tokens recur on other pages of their section less often than paragraph tokens of the same pages (zodiac 0.276 against 0.363; pharmaceutical 0.090 against 0.364; Table S19). Of 271 zodiac label types, 36 occur on two or more of the twelve sign pages and none on more than 4, so they are not numerals of days or degrees. Labels show low cross-page recurrence, consistent with mostly unique identifiers.

3.5 What the profile establishes

All tested structure is compatible with a local, low-order glyph process whose parameters drift by section, page and hand. Neither a lexicon nor information about the colours of the drawings is measurable beyond it. This is a statement of sufficiency: the automaton is a positive null model, not a historical procedure, and a cipher whose free choices are driven by a plaintext could leave the same footprint (Section 8). The profile leaves open which mechanisms the text actually excludes. The pre-specified program that follows addresses this and finds properties the automaton lacks: short-range recency, within-page drift, line-final forms that depend on whether the line ends at the margin, and very low recurrence of locally deviant forms.

4.2 Claims policy

Three rules were fixed before any test:

  • Statistical sufficiency of a model is not historical identification of a mechanism.
  • No result is reported as showing that the text is meaningless or contains no plaintext. The strongest admissible statement about the plaintext hypothesis is that a named variant of it makes a prediction that fails.
  • A concrete generator is reconstructed only if the verdict rule favours a fixed procedure.

4.3 Pre-specification and stages

We call an analysis pre-specified when its hypotheses, statistics, predictions, falsifiers and calibration requirements were written down and committed to the project's git repository before its Voynich outcome was computed. This is prospective specification with version control, not registration in an external registry such as OSF. The commits are listed in Table S28. The repository was private while the work was done and was published when it was complete, so the timestamps are the author's record and cannot be verified independently. Before publication the author e-mail address was removed from all commits; this changed the commit identities but not their contents or dates (mechanism/COMMIT_MAP.md). The battery ran in two stages: controls only (Stage A, committed), then the Voynich text (Stage B). Four further analyses answer a review of an earlier version (R1–R4, mechanism/REVISION_PLAN.md): R2–R4 were pre-specified before computation, and R1 is a post hoc check.

Deviations were logged with the reason and the time relative to the Voynich run (Table S27). All were made before the affected Voynich statistic was computed. The main ones:

  • T1 null model. The pre-specified corpus-wide re-pairing null for line-break coupling was confounded by writer heterogeneity. It was replaced by a page-conditional estimator that we validated on a known-truth simulation.
  • E1 expectation. Position-stratified expectations were added after a control without settings showed a spurious step.
  • E5 intervals. A page bootstrap was replaced by a quire jackknife, because duplicated pages bias same-page pair counts.

Limits of blindness. The author knew several Voynich properties from the exploratory profile (Section 3); they are declared in the battery's analysis plan. While diagnosing the T1 estimator, its terms were computed once on the Voynich text; that test's outcome was already known and is reported as "prior". After E3 the Voynich values of both halves had been computed, so later generator comparisons were blind only in their fitted parameters, not in the values they were compared with.

4.4 Calibration

A test counts toward a verdict only if it separates controls of the classes it is meant to separate (Table 3). Two pre-specified predictions were contradicted by their own controls and were narrowed rather than applied:

  • All four natural languages drift within a page. The within-page drift test (T6b) therefore separates only stationary procedures from the rest.
  • The Naibbe cipher reuses its rare forms as seldom as the procedures do. The deviant-token test (T7b) therefore excludes only word-preserving plaintext systems.

5. The pre-specified battery

5.1 Tests

  • T1, line-break transparency. The ratio of the excess mutual information between the last unit of a line and the first unit of the next to that between adjacent words inside lines. It is computed per page with a re-pairing null and averaged.
  • T2a, final form. The Jensen–Shannon divergence between the final-unit distribution of line-final words and that of line-internal words of the same length. It is computed separately for margin-bound and paragraph-final lines, against a permutation null.
  • T2b, line fill. Slack at the line end relative to greedy packing of the same page's words.
  • T2c, dittography. Repetition of words across line breaks.
  • T3a, zero replication. In matched 250-token chunks, how often unit bigrams that are absent in one half although expected (independence model) appear in the other half.
  • T3b, slot rigidity. The share of ordered unit pairs inside words that is consistent with the best single linear order of units.
  • T3c, positional entropy. Entropy of the unit at each position from the start and the end of the word.
  • T4, periodicity. Lag, column-alignment and line-cycle profiles, with power measured on a periodic device.
  • T5, drift shape. Step against smooth models of page-to-page distances within a hand.
  • T6a, recency. Exact and near (edit distance 1) repeats at short against long token distance, relative to within-page permutation.
  • T6b, within-page drift. The slope of line similarity over line distance.
  • T7a, annotated corrections.
  • T7b, deviant tokens. Double-coded tokens whose unit trigrams occur in no other quire, and whether they recur.

5.3 Verdict of the battery

Under the pre-specified scoring the fixed procedure collected the most support. It was vetoed by the recency test, because the text shows local dynamics that none of the tested stationary procedures produces. Improvisation was supported by recency and contradicted by the drift shape and the deviant forms. The tested plaintext variants each failed a test. The verdict was therefore a bounded, unresolved competition among three classes:

  • a procedure with state below the page level;
  • a practised improviser with an internalised hard grammar;
  • a plaintext-driven procedure with rigid tables and recency-dependent choices.

The experiments of Section 6 were designed to separate them.

6. Experiments that followed the battery

6.1 E1: settings or continuous recency?

Local dynamics can arise from settings that stay fixed within a line or paragraph and change at its boundary. They can also arise from continuous recency, in which a form becomes more likely after a similar form a few words earlier. The first predicts steps in pair similarity at boundaries and no decay inside a segment; the second predicts the reverse.

E1 compares pairs of line-internal words at matched distance within a line, across a line break, and across a paragraph break. Expectations are stratified by position class. Its graded line step is +0.054 (0.026, 0.080); line settings are excluded because the lower bound lies above the pre-specified threshold of −0.039. Its paragraph step is −0.039 (−0.102, 0.019); paragraph settings are excluded, marginally, because the lower bound lies above the threshold of −0.116. Decay inside lines exceeds every control (0.069 (0.037, 0.101) against −0.05 to 0.01). Both transliterations agree.

6.2 E2, E3 and E6: explicit generators on held-out pages

E2 built a generator of the surviving class:

  • a hard-pruned order-3 character automaton per group, restarted at each line;
  • a quire-level session weight and a page-level tilt of glyph preferences;
  • continuous recency;
  • slips.

It was fitted on the odd bifolios against five targets and tested on the even bifolios against 40 statistics. These are the profile statistics of Section 3, T1–T7 and E1. A statistic passes if the generator's mean lies within 2√(SE² + SD²) + 1% of the Voynich value, with SE from a quire jackknife and SD over three seeds.

E3 added the three components whose absence E2's failures pointed to:

  • separate line-end models for margin-bound and paragraph-final lines, with tight length control and a final word chosen so the line can end there;
  • a random walk of glyph preferences from line to line;
  • slips that are never reused, with memory keeping the intended form, plus a choice temperature.

E3 was cross-fitted: its primary direction was fitted on the even half and tested on the odd half, whose Voynich values for 35 of the 40 statistics had not been computed. 6.3 E4 and E5: where the drift and the page effects live

E4 decomposes the within-page drift into a lexical part, meaning which word types two lines share, and a sublexical part, meaning how the remaining words are built. For the sublexical part, words within edit distance 2 of each other are removed from the comparison. Slopes are taken over line distances 2–8, which excludes token-level recency.

The Voynich drift is lexical in type (L = −0.0142 (−0.0275, −0.0022)) with a small sublexical part (S = −0.0052 (−0.0085, −0.0023)). That is the profile of plain Latin (−0.0028) and German (−0.0043). The pre-specified bar for "sublexical beyond content drift" was set by Dante's terza rima (−0.0153), and it was not met, so E4 does not separate content drift from vocabulary or habit drift.

A comparison of pre-specified statistics adds one point. Both ciphers of Latin remove the sublexical drift that their plaintext has (deterministic verbose cipher +0.0002 (−0.0020, 0.0022); Naibbe −0.0015). The Voynich interval lies below the deterministic cipher's, so letter-level ciphers of the tested kinds are poor sources of the observed drift.

E5 asks whether the page clustering of word types is content-like. Content drift makes rare content words the burstiest (Church and Gale 1995): a name or term clusters on the pages about its subject. E5 compares observed same-page pairs with their expectation under permutation within hand × section, by frequency band. The gradient G contrasts rare (2–4) with frequent (20–99) types.

The languages give G = +0.74 to +1.08, the deterministic cipher +0.66 and Naibbe +0.07. The Voynich value is +0.245 (−0.099, 0.588) (Takahashi +0.189), which is undetermined by the pre-specified rule; E5 is therefore used only descriptively. Its point estimate lies below the lower bound of every content control. It clusters by page in every band, including forms occurring at least 100 times (1.36 (1.17, 1.56) against 1.02–1.22 in the languages).

So the page effect sits in the familiar forms, which natural-language content drift leaves nearly uniform. A glyph-level generator with page tilts reproduces the clustering of frequent forms but overshoots on rare ones (B = 3.68 for rare forms, against 2.02). Part of what earlier work read as keyword clustering (Montemurro and Zanette 2013) may be this production-level page effect. Our test measures clustering by page within hand and section, not their section-level measure, so the two are not directly comparable.

8. The message is not identifiable from the text (E7)

8.1 Construction

The mechanism class is defined by observed properties, so it could in principle also contain deterministic mechanisms. The argument here does not need the whole class. It needs processes that make free choices: at each glyph several continuations are allowed with known probabilities. Every explicit generator of Sections 3 and 6 is of this kind, and a message can be carried by making its choices depend on the message.

The principle is not new. Wayner (1992) introduced mimic functions, and steganography theory shows that a message encoded to be indistinguishable from random bits can drive the random choices of a known channel without changing its output distribution (Cachin 2004; Hopper, Langford and von Ahn 2002). What E7 adds is a construction for a Voynich-like process on the manuscript's own layout, a measured capacity, and a test against the complete phenotype used throughout this paper. The rate-optimal way to do this is arithmetic decoding, a mimic function in the sense of Wayner (1992). The message bits are read as a binary fraction. At each choice the current interval is divided in proportion to the process's probabilities, and the sub-interval that contains the fraction is taken. A receiver who knows the process re-encodes the observed choices and obtains the bits.

We implemented this with the 32-bit coder of Witten, Neal and Cleary (1987), applied to the order-3 glyph automaton of the battery on the Voynich layout. Three conditions were compared, each through the full 40-statistic phenotype:

  • random driving (3 seeds);
  • compressed plaintexts: Latin, Italian and German, compressed with lzma;
  • the same plaintexts as raw UTF-8 bits.

Proposition. Let a process emit a sequence of categorical choices with probabilities pt(· | history) known to sender and receiver. Let the choices be made by arithmetic decoding of a bit sequence U.

  1. If U consists of independent, uniformly distributed bits, the output has the distribution of the process itself, up to the coder's finite precision. Hence every statistic of the text has the same distribution with and without a message.
  2. A receiver who knows the pt recovers U over the consumed length.
  3. The number of bits carried equals the log-loss of the generated text under the process, whose expectation is the process's entropy.

Sketch. Arithmetic decoding partitions [0, 1) into intervals whose lengths are the probabilities of the corresponding choice sequences. A uniformly distributed fraction falls into each with exactly that probability (Witten et al. 1987). Recovery follows because encoding is the inverse map on the consumed prefix. With a 32-bit range and the model counts used here, the per-choice probability error is far below the sampling noise of any statistic. Compressed data are close to independent uniform bits. The same principle underlies information-theoretic and complexity-theoretic definitions of secure steganography: a message encoded to be indistinguishable from uniform randomness can drive the random choices of a known channel without changing its distribution (Cachin 2004; Hopper, Langford and von Ahn 2002).

8.3 What this does and does not show

E7 does not show that the Voynich text carries a message. It shows that the question cannot be settled from the text, for this class of encoders. Any fully specified stochastic process with non-zero conditional entropy and known choice probabilities can in principle be driven by a message without changing its output distribution. For the Voynich conclusion it suffices that we constructed two pairs of processes, one carrying a message and one not, that the 40 statistics cannot tell apart. A message is identifiable only under a restriction on the encoder.

Within the restrictions tested here, the evidence is negative:

  • systems that preserve lexical identity at the token boundaries produce recurring locally deviant forms, of which the text has very few;
  • the Naibbe cipher lacks the recency, the boundary coupling and the page effects, also on its author's own ciphertext;
  • the two tested letter-level ciphers remove the sublexical drift that the text keeps.

Arithmetic coding is a twentieth-century technique. An encoder of equivalent effect in the fifteenth century would be a historical claim requiring historical evidence. Untested historical encoders may exist.

The same argument covers the other unresolved alternative. A written rule set with a randomiser and a practised writer's habits are described by the same generator, and no text statistic separates them.

9. Discussion

9.1 What is new

Relative to earlier work, the contributions are these:

  1. From the exploratory profile: the lexicon test; the compression residual against a drifting twin; a sufficient glyph process tested on held-out pages; the failure of ciphertext-feedback substitution and of state-dependent Naibbe variants, in which boundary coupling and hidden plaintext redundancy compete; the comparison of hand and section at fixed folio distance; and a text–illustration test with a calibrated sensitivity.
  2. A pre-specified, calibrated separation of production hypotheses, with human gibberish as the improvisation control at the level of constraints and line structure. Gaskell and Bowern (2022) compared low-level statistics and noted that their samples were too short for page structure.
  3. Line-final forms associated with the margin and much weaker at paragraph ends. Currier (1976) already described the line as a functional unit; the margin/paragraph contrast and its persistence on text-only pages are new.
  4. Continuous recency across line and paragraph boundaries, which excludes settings that switch per line or paragraph. It is weaker than, and structurally different from, the copying of Timm and Schinner (2020): there are no propagating innovations and no vertical copying.
  5. Very low recurrence of locally deviant forms, which separates the text from the language controls and from every generator we built, though not from the Naibbe cipher.
  6. Page clustering that extends to the most frequent forms, with a rare-type gradient below every content control.
  7. Held-out generator tests with ablations that turn "sufficient" into "necessary components plus two open specifics".
  8. A Voynich-specific constructive non-identifiability result: a measured capacity for a Voynich-like process and a test against the full phenotype. The underlying principle is that of mimic functions and secure steganography (Section 8.1).

The rigidity measure quantifies the slot structure described by Stolfi (2000) and Zattera (2022). The boundary coupling and the predictability of spaces confirm Rozanova and Temerev (2026) with different estimators. The long-range structure reported by Montemurro and Zanette (2013) corresponds, in our measurements, to drift of the glyph statistics that follows the scribal hand more than the section (Section 3.4) and to the page effect of E5. The positional entropy collapse (T3c: Voynich 0.63, Naibbe 0.65, automaton 0.67, languages 0.78–0.91, gibberish 0.98) is reproduced by verbose ciphers, as Kinnison (2026) reported. It separates the text from natural language and naive gibberish, not procedure from cipher.

9.2 Why text statistics cannot settle the plaintext question

The debate between "meaningless" and "cipher" readings has often been conducted with statistics: entropy, Zipf's law, word lengths, keyword clustering. The proposition in Section 8 explains why no statistic of this kind can end it. A message compressed and carried by arithmetic decoding leaves the text distribution unchanged. The only statistics that can reveal a message are those that detect a departure of the text from the distribution of the message-free process, and such a test presupposes a model of the encoder.

Evidence for or against a message must therefore take one of three forms:

  • tests against a specific, historically motivated encoder class, as done here for systems that preserve lexical identity and for the Naibbe cipher. Other historical systems need tests of their own. The Polygraphia III (Hermes 2022) is an obvious candidate: it encodes each plaintext letter as one invented token, and its tables and a simulation are public. Hermes compared it on entropy, word lengths and near-identical neighbours; whether it can also produce the boundary dependency (C2), the recency (C4) and the page variation (C5) found here is untested. It is also about a century younger than the manuscript;
  • external information, such as a key, a bilingual passage, or a demonstrable link between text and image, of which Section 3.4 found none at the tested sensitivity;
  • physical and historical evidence about how the manuscript was made.

This is not special to the Voynich manuscript. It is a property of every text produced by a stochastic process with free choices. It extends the conclusion of Bowern and Gaskell (2022), that unusual character-level statistics are no evidence that the text is gibberish: no statistic computed from the text can decide the question without a model of the encoder.

9.3 Rule, habit or message

Within the class, three considerations weigh without deciding.

  • No periodicity of the tested kind. There is no device footprint at the power we could test, for the one device design we tested.
  • Human production plausibility. Recency, page-level variation, margin-associated endings and drift are what one expects of a human writer, and a rule set would have to specify each explicitly, including a continuously drifting preference. Our own human control is too short to show recency or drift, so this consideration rests on general grounds, not on our data.
  • No tested cipher fits. Every historical-style cipher we tested leaves traces that the text lacks.

The most parsimonious reading is writers composing on the page within a strict learned grammar, choosing forms under short-term recency and page-dependent preferences, and choosing each line's last word with the margin in view. We label this a parsimony judgement, not a finding. Whether a message rode on those choices remains undecidable from the text.

9.4 Transmission

Copying from a re-lined draft is excluded (T2a). No copying signature was found: no dittography, and no word-level corrections among the annotated ones. The final forms depend on whether a line ends at the margin. The simplest interpretation is that the last token was chosen knowing where that line ends, that is, composition on the page. Copying from a draft with identical lines and margins cannot be excluded from the transliteration.

10. Limitations

  • Blindness is procedural, not epistemic. The author knew many Voynich properties before designing the tests, and both halves' values were known before E4–E6. The analysis plans are timestamped by commits in a repository that was private until the work was complete; there was no external registration.
  • The profile is exploratory. Section 3 was not specified in advance. Its table-and-grille and copy-and-modify generators are reconstructions of published descriptions that could not be checked against their authors' outputs; the Naibbe reimplementation was checked against its author's published ciphertext (R2). The illustration test uses crude colour features of folios 1–58 only, and its sensitivity (about a tenth of a page's tokens) bounds what it can exclude.
  • Two transliterations, one source. ZL3b and IT2a are independent readings of the same images; transliteration conventions (spaces, ligatures) remain a shared source of error.
  • Controls.
    • The human gibberish is short, modern and in the Latin alphabet; no data exist on long-practised improvisation in an invented script, which is the main reason the executor question stays open.
    • The language controls are modern editions, and none is a heavily abbreviated medieval text.
    • Most generator controls were fitted to the Voynich text.
    • The Vulgate control was lost to an empty file.
  • Estimator dependence.
    • The line-break ratio ranges from about 0.03 (pooled estimator) to 0.22 (page-conditional estimator, the only one valid under writer heterogeneity).
    • The hardness contrast depends on sample size.
    • T4's power is known only for the tested device design (period 5, cycle 4).
  • Page order is the binding order, which departs from the writing order in places. This weakens T5 and T4c.
  • Corrections rest on 24 annotations made for reading purposes; line fill uses glyph counts, not physical widths.
  • Sufficiency of the class is not demonstrated: the best generator reproduces 33 of 40 statistics.
  • E7 and E8 check the proposition for two processes, the order-3 automaton and the E3 generator. The proposition covers any process with known choice probabilities; E8 carries the message only in observable choices, so its capacity is a lower bound for that process.
  • The 40 statistics are correlated and assessed with a tolerance rule, not a joint test.
  • Analysis changes after calibration. Several estimators were changed after their calibration failed: the T1 null model, the E1 expectations and the E5 intervals (Table S27). Every change was made before the affected Voynich statistic was computed. The T1 terms were computed once on the Voynich text during the diagnosis.
  • Undetermined results. E5 was undetermined by its pre-specified rule, and E4 did not separate content drift from habit drift. The paper uses them only descriptively and not as evidence for any exclusion.
  • Tokens and words. Whether Voynich tokens are words is itself open (Section 3.2). Exclusions phrased in terms of lexical identity refer to identity at the transcribed token boundaries.

11. Conclusion

An exploratory profile shows that a local, low-order glyph process with drifting parameters reproduces most of the measured statistics of the Voynich text, and that neither a lexicon nor information about the colours of the drawings is measurable beyond it. Pre-specified, calibrated tests then narrow the production of the text to a class defined by nine measured properties. The text excludes:

  • naive improvisation;
  • the tested stationary procedures (tables, grilles, automata) without a recency state;
  • periodic devices of the tested design;
  • settings that switch per line or paragraph;
  • copy-and-modify with propagating innovations;
  • the tested natural-language controls, systems that preserve lexical identity at the token boundaries, and the Naibbe cipher as published;
  • copying from a re-lined draft.

It leaves three alternatives that it cannot decide between: an explicit rule set, a practised writer's habits, and a message carried by the free choices. For the last, we showed constructively that a message can be present without leaving any trace in the statistics used here. For uniformly distributed message bits, it leaves a trace in no statistic that can be computed from the text.

A set of production constraints can be recovered from the text, but whether the free choices of the constrained process carry a message is not identifiable from the text alone. Progress beyond this point requires evidence the text does not contain:

  • long-term improvisation and rule-execution experiments with human writers;
  • physical examination of the manuscript for session boundaries and corrections;
  • historical sources describing a procedure of this kind.
